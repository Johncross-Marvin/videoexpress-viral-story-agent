# Viral-Pattern Story Engine — Mandatory Scriptwriting Specification

This file is part of the VideoExpress Long-Form Multi-Character Agent. Read and apply it **before** creating the story bible, image prompts, animation clips or timeline. The two user-supplied analyses are **pattern references**, not transcripts to copy, proof of future views, or mandatory themes. Write an **original fictional story** whose visual promises are actually paid off.

## 1. Goal: cinematic, emotionally legible retention

Write 5–20 minutes of coherent storytelling that earns attention through conflict, empathy, useful progression, escalating consequences, visual and sensory specificity, and satisfying closure. The target is **story completion**, not misleading cliffhangers or needless cruelty. Every scene must reveal something, change a situation, achieve a concrete step, or change how a character understands the problem. Never prolong an obvious resolution just to pad minutes.

A strong opening should introduce an immediately intelligible **change in circumstances or striking discovery** within 0–10 seconds, with no channel welcome, logo, recitation of the title, long backstory, or generic moral. For stories that benefit from a warm domestic moment, allow up to ~20 seconds of meaningful everyday behavior before disruption, provided the title or first visual already establishes compelling stakes. Establish the core promise or problem in the first minute.

Choose an **engine** based on the user's premise. If the user names an engine, follow it. If not, select the engine that naturally fits the story, record why, and continue without an extra approval step.

### Engine A — INJUSTICE_TO_TRANSFORMATION

Use for redemption through visible work: an abandoned vehicle, neglected storefront, ruined garden, damaged workshop, broken family heirloom, neglected public space, lost opportunity, or a community rebuilding project. Replace the specific example's cast, location, objects, plot sequence and wording with original creative choices.

**Emotional spine:** unjust loss / rejection → physical consequence → discovery of a neglected possibility → specific vow → observable step-by-step work → a surprising *it works* moment → warmth/usefulness/ownership → dignity restored → credible next-day proof.

1. **Injustice/consequence (0–10%):** Make unfairness visible or plainly understood. Show who is hurt and what is actually lost; do not caricature the antagonist if complexity is useful.
2. **Discovery and vow (~5–17%):** Introduce an undervalued place or object with measurable defects (three concrete sensory details). Let a character commit to a *specific attainable change*, not a generic speech.
3. **Early micro-win (~12–25%):** Show the first completed task and a visibly changed before/after frame. The viewer must see that the project can progress.
4. **Transformation ladder (~20–70%):** Move through varied steps, each with action → resistance/constraint → visible completion. Use the right causal sequence for the real task (cleaning, repair, structures, systems, function, comfort), but do not fake dangerous expertise or demonstrate unsafe repairs as if reliable.
5. **Functional reveal (~40–65%):** A test proves a critical feature works (lights, water, an engine if plausibly repaired, first customer served, restored instrument sounding). This is a second climax, not the end.
6. **Human use (~60–90%):** Move from a machine/object to a person's need: rest, food, belonging, work, care, community. The first meal/first restful night/first customer is a ceremony of earned progress.
7. **Dignity payoff and durability (~80–100%):** Echo the opening injustice briefly, let action—not a long revenge monologue—show the reversal, address the last practical concern, and close with a credible next-day/afterward proof and meaningful relationship or identity beat.

**Natural sensory tools:** scraping rust, bucket splash, work gloves, brush on wood, click of a switch, rain on a roof, kettle steam, simmering food, morning light. Choose only details fitting the setting; use the same landmark and ambient audio to tie several sequences together. Name a solved problem when visibly solved. Do not invent completed safety guarantees or skip essential conditions for livability.

### Engine B — SACRIFICE_TO_JUSTICE

Use for family, friendship or community stories where someone quietly pays a cost and their truth or worth is later recognized. Write original motives and relationships, not the source example's official, brick kiln, pregnancy, family festival, or dialogue unless supplied independently by the user.

**Emotional spine:** human bond → sacrifice with a hidden cost → public/private injustice → mounting concrete consequences → a glimmer of help → believable setback → a deadline or decision point → revelation and earned intervention → accountability → emotionally complex repair or release.

1. **Set the bond and disruption (0–12%):** An understandable domestic exchange or immediate confrontation. Introduce a tangible unmet need: debt paper, empty medicine tin, job notice, missing cheque, broken phone, or similar prop. A line may plant a question without withholding essential clarity artificially.
2. **Reveal the sacrifice (5–25%):** The protagonist chooses a real cost for someone else's goal. The motive can be revealed a little later, but the reveal must reinterpret an event already seen.
3. **Escalate by consequence (20–70%):** Two or more distinct obstacles, not repetitions of a villain shouting. Each escalation alters money, safety, reputation, freedom, relationship or options. Show the cost in an observable behavior or object.
4. **Hope valve and setback (~45–75%):** A realistic person, message, clue or small success makes justice or escape plausible. A causal obstacle delays it. Never make characters irrational just to preserve a reveal.
5. **Deadline / lowest point (~65–85%):** Bring forward a genuinely relevant appointment, weather change, inspection, payment date, ceremony or personal decision. Do not fabricate a countdown for every story.
6. **Earned reversal (~75–92%):** Seed the evidence, ally, professional role or character growth **before** the intervention; the title promise begins paying off through identifiable actions. Competent characters make proportionate decisions; do not depict instant arrests or legal outcomes without plausible procedures.
7. **Accountability and aftermath (~88–100%):** Clarify what happens to harm-doers and what is restored. Forgiveness is optional, never compulsory; reunion, independence, accountability or bittersweet acceptance can all be truthful endings. Close on the protagonist's restored agency, not merely a rescuer's rank.

**Natural emotional tools:** an altered family photograph, letter, skipped meal, recurring appointment, cherished field or object, restrained dialogue, overheard truth, a quiet reversal of an earlier line. Give the central sufferer agency and complexity; avoid exploiting prolonged humiliation, disability, pregnancy, poverty or vulnerable people for empty spectacle.

### Engine C — HYBRID_REDEMPTION

Use only when the user story naturally contains **both** a visible build/transformation and a sacrifice/recognition arc. Designate one as **primary** and the other as supporting. The climax must resolve the main character's want, not simply show a makeover and then append an unrelated rescue. A build result may *be* the evidence that corrects an injustice.

## 2. Story-promise contract, title and thumbnail brief

Before scripting, define:
- **Who we care about**, what they want, why now, who or what blocks it, what concrete sacrifice/loss makes it matter.
- **One-sentence viewer promise**: a question the ending will answer on screen.
- **Title hook**: 1–2 natural clauses combining a concrete threat/contrast with a believable transformation or revelation; numbers and emotion only when true in the script. Title must not promise a scene that is absent.
- **Thumbnail concept**: one instantly readable moment or contrast (before/after for A; danger vs credible hope/revelation for B), legible at mobile size, matching the characters and finished video. The thumbnail is a design brief, not an automatically generated image unless requested.
- **Primary open loop** opened in the title/first 20 seconds, closed by the final act; **secondary loops** each resolved after a satisfying interval. Do not constantly stack new unanswered questions or misrepresent the ending.
- **Final emotional receipt**: the precise, filmable frame or line that proves the promise. Build toward that; write it before planning the middle.

Title patterns to adapt without copying:
- A: `[Concrete setback]! [Who] Turns [Neglected Thing] into [Meaningful New Use]`.
- B: `[Who] Returns / Discovers [Person] Facing [Specific Consequence] — [Earned Revelation]`.
- C: `[Loss] Forced [Who] to Rebuild [Thing] — Then [Truth / Unexpected Result]`.

Treat high-view-count examples as hypotheses about attention, not formulas that guarantee virality. Avoid spammy emoji overload, invented facts, fabricated footage of real events, and inaccurate authority or medical claims.

## 3. Time architecture: scale the emotional beats to 5–20 minutes

**These are elastic windows, not mandatory timestamps.** Script the requested length and use the model's actually available clip duration for segmentation. Any imported 11- or 27-minute example must be **re-timed**, not copied wholesale or stretched beyond the 20-minute maximum.

| Phase | Typical range of final runtime | Needed viewer experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cold open / disruption | 0–4% (first 0–10 s preferred) | What happened? Why is it unfair / intriguing? |
| Consequence + personal stakes | 4–12% | Who pays the price, what will happen if nothing changes? |
| Discovery, sacrifice or vow | 10–22% | A clear direction and promise |
| Visible progress / escalating obstacles | 20–45% | Micro-payoffs and cost, never repetitive filler |
| New information / functional midpoint | 40–65% | A surprising competent success, reveal or changed goal |
| Harder problem / hope reversal | 55–78% | Greater stakes without random cruelty |
| Lowest point + meaningful decision | 68–85% | Someone actively chooses what matters |
| Primary promise payoff / reversal | 78–92% | Answer the title's core question visibly |
| Practical and emotional closure | 88–100% | Accountability, lasting change, quiet proof, one resonant last image |

For a **5-minute story**, combine beats and deliver the first micro-payoff within roughly the first minute; do not force 10 separate transformation phases. For **10–12 minutes**, support several tasks or emotional turns and one strong midpoint. For **15–20 minutes**, deepen relationship arcs, introduce meaningful second-order consequences and allow more earned setbacks. Every new subplot must change the outcome; do not use suffering or painting montages merely to consume runtime.

**Pacing targets (adapt to genre):**
- Make the first interesting visual/cause appear within **0–10 seconds**; set problem/promise clearly by **0:30–1:00**.
- Give a distinct **micro-payoff or material new obstacle every ~45–90 seconds** in a build saga, or a **new consequence, revelation or decision every ~60–120 seconds** in a family drama. The beat should change the story, not merely restate it.
- Build a purposeful **midpoint pattern interrupt**, ideally at **40–65%**: a machine starts, first paying customer arrives, secret message is discovered, evidence reaches an ally, or the character changes strategy.
- Tie recurring sensory/action details to plot functions: a waterfall can be landscape + washing source + view + sound bed, but only where logically consistent.
- Use first-time milestones sparingly and sincerely: first functioning light, first customer, first peaceful meal, first night without fear.
- The title promise gets at least a **partial satisfying payment before the last few seconds**; use the final portion to show durable consequence, not merely an abrupt CTA.

## 4. Script voice: simple, vivid, emotionally credible

**Write the complete production script before generating media.** Use third-person present tense for action and natural spoken dialogue in the characters' chosen language and accent. When narration is chosen, write **filmable, image-supporting narration**, not a constant explanation of what the viewer can already see. Prioritize familiar, conversational sentences, concrete verbs, sensory cues, and character intent; write for listening as well as watching.

- **Cold-open dialogue** should be short, specific and charged with consequence. Examples of *new, original* lines: “We lock this workshop at sundown.” / “Then give me until the bell rings.” These are illustrative; invent lines fitted to each plot.
- **Dialogue reveals character:** one person threatens to take a specific action, another makes a choice, someone else hides or reveals a cost. Keep distinct vocabulary, speech pace and tone per character. Avoid long monologues that a 10-second animation cannot contain.
- **Announce earned completions** through action or brief line: “The roof finally held through the rain.” Never say “safe forever” because of an implausibly brief repair.
- **Sensory immersion** ties to actions and continuity: buzzing fluorescent light during night work, a soft metallic click, hot oil hissing, wet footsteps, the same passing train sound. Specify one or two salient sounds per short clip; avoid meaningless sonic clutter.
- **Co-laboring voice** (Engine A) may occasionally use “Come on, one more panel,” or “Now the floor,” but do not have a narrator command viewers in every scene.
- **Emotion through action:** a hand hovering over a foreclosure letter, an uneaten plate, a daughter folding her father's work gloves, a newly warm room. Avoid repeatedly labeling someone “devastated” without a visual cause.
- **Moral reversal:** echo a detail from the opening with changed meaning (“He said nobody would use this place again. Listen to the music coming from inside.”), without requiring a cruel revenge speech.
- **No artificial escalation:** don't repeat abuse beats, rely on stereotypes, or make every antagonist cartoonishly evil. Consequences should be motivated and believable. Match law, medicine and technical details to defensible reality; if uncertain, write a simpler, nontechnical outcome rather than an unsupported claim.
- **No copied material:** Do not reproduce the source stories' distinctive dialogue, names, setting-specific festival, scene order or text. Borrow the abstract dramatic mechanics only. Keep characters and outcomes original.

### Speech and clip-budget discipline

A 5–20-minute finished runtime is the **sum of verified clip and audio durations**, adjusted for overlaps/trims; it is not necessarily 5–20 minutes of nonstop speech.

- Narrated drama: target roughly **110–145 spoken words/minute** when narration fills much of the video. Slower emotional pauses lower the actual count. Do **not** take the raw formula `minutes × words/min` as mandatory; scenes with action and ambience need fewer spoken words.
- Ten-second clip with in-clip dialogue: usually **10–20 spoken words total**, including all speakers; one meaningful line or exchange is safer than paragraphs. At 120 wpm, 20 words alone use ~10 seconds with **no movement or silence**, so shorten when the clip has pauses.
- Write silence as a deliberate storytelling beat. Let assembly-level narrator/audio tracks handle extended speech when visibly supported. Never fake synchronized voice capabilities or use a new random voice in each clip.
- For an audio-podcast style, maintain a stable narrator and speaker voices and frequent meaningful visual changes. Include adequate captions only if requested/supported, not fictitious UI features.

## 5. Required outputs BEFORE asset generation

Produce these planning artifacts internally or in project files. Present a synopsis for user review only if asked; routine story generation does not need repeated approvals.

1. **Story engine selection** (`INJUSTICE_TO_TRANSFORMATION`, `SACRIFICE_TO_JUSTICE`, or `HYBRID_REDEMPTION`) and why it fits.
2. **Story package:** title candidates (prefer strongest as working title), one-sentence promise, thumbnail visual concept, genre/setting/audience, empathy anchor, protagonist want and agency, antagonist/obstacle, stakes, moral/revelation, planned final emotional receipt.
3. **Hook test:** exact first image and spoken words; identify the conflict/curiosity within first 0–10 s, title question, and the scene where it is answered.
4. **Beat sheet:** every beat has a beat ID, start/end timeline seconds, `cause → action → change`, emotional shift, tangible prop or image, open-loop opened/resolved, and a micro-payoff or escalation label. Timings must cover intended duration, no dead zones.
5. **Full timed script:** for every scene or block, provide visual action, recurring character IDs, exact dialogue and/or narration with speaker names, time budget, location, SFX/ambience, outgoing continuity state, and any cut/transition. Avoid placeholders such as “the next 3 minutes are a montage.” If a build takes time, describe its observable stages and distinct completion frames.
6. **Retention audit:** first 10 s, first 60 s, at least one earned midpoint event, estimated interval between changed stakes/micro-payoffs, clear title promise payoff, last unresolved practical concern, honest ending. Verify no filler, recycled scene, misleading promise or forced suffering.
7. **Duration/voice audit:** spoken words vs available scene seconds; total scripted scene durations equal target *before editing*; no two character references exceed the documented UI capacity per clip. Record likely need for trim buffers or extra scenes so final measured export remains within 5:00–20:00.
8. **Production handoff:** only after the full script passes quality checks should the storyboard expand into starting-image prompts and the Video-and-Audio prompts used in VideoExpress. Each short clip must have a single physical action, relevant emotion and an outgoing frame that matches the next shot.

### Required per-scene writing schema

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `scene_id`, `beat_id`, `start_s`, `end_s` | Unique shot and meaningful timed story beat |
| `story_function` | Hook, sacrifice, discovery, micro-win, reversal, midpoint, low point, payoff, aftermath |
| `viewer_question` | What the audience needs answered now |
| `change` | What is observably different at the end of the shot |
| `on_screen_character_ids`, `location_id` | Cast/set bible references; max two individual refs per clip unless UI proves otherwise |
| `visual_action` | Filmable behavior, prop state and emotional physical cue |
| `dialogue` / `narration` | Exact spoken words, attributed to stable voices |
| `ambience` / `sfx` | Sounds supporting scene continuity |
| `open_loop_id` / `payoff_id` | Track promises from opening to fulfillment |
| `incoming_state` / `outgoing_state` / `transition_to_next` | Continuity needed for generation and edit |

## 6. Production conversion without losing the script

Start with the approved full script's chronology, not whatever order media cards render. Convert each scene into the available short-duration animated clips, often 10 seconds, or split a long scene across multiple clips. Do not compress 50 seconds of dialogue into one 10-second prompt, nor generate dozens of similar people staring blankly while narration repeats. Use filmable story actions, prop inserts, motivated reaction shots and varying shot sizes to maintain a living cinematic story.

Keep the selected 3D adult animation or hyperreal human mode fixed; all character faces, voices, locations, clothing changes and relevant props use the Story Bible. The project must use the user's exact `Media Library → My Media → [folder]` where the interface supports the route, while final assembly occurs in a **separate clean timeline project**. Confirm actual controls and saved assets in the visible browser. Script quality does not override permissions, credit limits, account access, generation queues, edit continuity or export verification.

## 7. Do not misrepresent success

You may say a package **uses techniques found in the supplied viral analyses**, but not that it “guarantees 10 million views,” “beats the algorithm,” or replicates either film. If a premise doesn't suit humiliation/revenge, choose a gentler visual discovery or relationship engine. The winning criterion is an authentic, satisfying story that fulfills the title and looks/sounds consistent—not a formula that makes claims about actual platform recommendation.
